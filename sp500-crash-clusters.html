S&P 500 crash clusters

Thirteen drawdowns since 1990, grouped by how fast and how long they fall. The group decides which family of hedge trigger can arrive in time, and which will always be late.

  • 13 episodes
  • 1990–2026 window
  • 15% depth threshold
  • 4 groups
  • Daily closes to 15 Sep 2026

No single rule fits every crash. A hedge tuned on extended bear markets loses money in fast corrections, and neither reaches a flash shock before it is over. The useful question is not which trigger is best, but which group you want to hedge, and what that costs each year.

Four groups

Time to a new high is measured from the low of each episode.

Flash shocks

Under 25 sessions, over 1% a day

Covid, February–March 2020

New high after 5 months

No price-based trigger arrives in time. The trend breaks its moving average when most of the move has already happened. Either the hedge is already in the portfolio, or you accept not hedging.

Fast corrections

About 30–65 sessions, 0.3–0.6% a day

Gulf War 1990 · LTCM 1998 · 2010 · Euro debt 2011 · Q4 2018 · Tariffs 2025

New high after 3–5 months

The hardest ground. The trigger enters mid-decline, and the sharp recovery hands back the gain on the hedge. What matters here is the exit rule, not the entry.

Extended bears

Over 190 sessions, under 0.2% a day

Dot-com 2000–02 · Financial crisis 2007–09 · Rates and inflation 2022

New high after 15–56 months

Trend following’s territory. Months below the moving average repay the false signals many times over. It is the only group where a full 100% hedge makes sense.

Controls

Under the 15% threshold, kept out of the sample

Volmageddon 2018 · Carry-trade unwind 2024 · China and oil 2015–16

New high after 2–6 months

They measure false positives. These are the cases where a hedge switches on, pays its cost and never finds the crisis it was waiting for.

What the study does not show

  • The window starts in 1990. Black Monday, 1987, is left out. It is the fastest crash on record and would sit with the flash shocks.
  • The flash-shock group is one case. Covid is the only drawdown over 15% that falls that fast in the window, so read the group as an example, not a statistic.
  • Thirteen episodes is a small sample, and the group boundaries are set by hand on speed and length, not found by an algorithm.
  • The controls are hand-picked. Three sub-threshold episodes are shown; other declines of 10–12% (1997, 1999, 2023) are not included, so the false-positive count is a sample, not a census.
  • Numbers are recomputed on SPY closing prices, used as a proxy for the index from 1993. Depths can differ from the index itself by a few tenths of a point, mostly because of dividends. The 1990 Gulf War episode predates the series and was not recomputed.

The distance between the groups is the result that matters. A rule calibrated on the extended bears loses systematically in the fast corrections, and neither touches the flash shocks. The practical conclusion is not to pick the best trigger, but to decide which group to hedge and at what annual cost.
