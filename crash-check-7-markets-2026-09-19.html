Crash check on seven markets

The test we ran on the S&P 500, applied to the seven Hedge Gauge markets: how close each one is to the phases in the Crash Clusters study, and the odds for the week of 21–25 September.

  • 7 markets
  • 13 reference episodes
  • 1990–2026 history
  • Closes to 18 Sep 2026

None of the seven markets is inside one of the four groups today. Weekly risk is highest in Italy, the Nasdaq 100 and Europe, where a 3% fall at some close during the week is 14–16% likely, and lowest in the S&P 500, the UK and Australia, at 7–8%. Much of that gap is volatility, not a worse setup: the macro backdrop of rising yields and $100 oil is common to all seven.

How the numbers are built. For each market, three estimates of the chance of a −3%, −5% and −10% close within five sessions: a model on 21-day realised volatility alone, a model that adds distance from the high, the 200-day average, 21-day return and the 10-year yield change, and the historical frequency in days with similar volatility above the 200-day. The three are averaged, then scaled by 1.4 for the −3% column and 2 for the −5% and −10% columns to reflect the unusual macro backdrop (yields and oil rising faster than at past tops). The S&P 500 row is the same as in the S&P 500 Crash Check. The “episode starts” column is the market’s own weekly base rate, the average of its 1990–2026 and 2003–2026 rates, multiplied by 1.7. Both multipliers are judgements, not fitted values. The VIX-implied one-week move for the S&P 500 is ±2.05%; the Nasdaq 100 VXN, at 19.3, implies ±2.7%. No implied-volatility index was available for the European and Australian markets.

Are the falls so far the start of a real episode?

The same test used on the S&P 500. At the same number of sessions after its own high, how many of the ten real episodes had already fallen further than the market has today?

The higher the bar, the less today’s fall looks like the opening weeks of a real episode. The S&P 500 sits at the top: all ten real episodes were already deeper at this age. Germany is the exception, at 4 of 10: its 4.8% fall in 15 sessions is typical of an episode’s first weeks, but it is also what ordinary pullbacks look like, so it is not conclusive. For the FTSE 100 and Nasdaq 100 the high is 140 and 75 sessions old, and the test is weaker.

Hedge Gauge alongside this check

  • Italy40 scores highest on both: 76% on Hedge Gauge and the highest weekly odds here.
  • UK100 is 76% on Hedge Gauge but has one of the lowest weekly odds and the lowest volatility of the European markets.
  • Australia50 is the lowest on Hedge Gauge, 22%, and the weakest chart of the seven, the only one below its 200-day average.

Hedge Gauge is a proprietary algorithm based on momentum and pairwise correlation, on a longer horizon than this one-week check, so the two need not agree. UK100 and Australia50 are the two markets where the readings diverge most.

Method and limits

  • Episodes are defined as in the S&P 500 study: a fall of at least 15% from a peak, which ends when the market regains the peak or rebounds 25% from the low. On S&P 500 data since 1990 this rule reproduces exactly the ten episodes of the study. Groups follow the study: under 25 sessions and over 1% a day, flash shock; under 120 sessions, fast correction; longer, extended bear.
  • Base rates differ a lot by market. Weekly base rates for a new ≥15% episode range from 0.5% (S&P 500, FTSE 100) to 1.2% (FTSE MIB). The Nasdaq 100 rate is inflated by the 1990s: 22 episodes since 1990, of which 10 since 2003.
  • The seven markets are not independent. They tend to fall together, so the odds above cannot be combined as if they were separate bets.
  • Indices are in local currency, not in the USD ETFs used by Hedge Gauge, so currency moves are not included. Data are Yahoo Finance index closes to 18 September 2026; Europe50 starts in 2007, Italy40 in 1997 and Australia50 in 1992.
  • The macro backdrop is the one described in the S&P 500 Crash Check, taken from news reports and not checked against primary releases: Fed hike on 16 September, US 10-year at 5.00%, WTI around $100 and the US–Iran conflict. The economic calendar for 21–25 September was not verified.

Rising yields and oil affect all seven markets, but the setup differs: the US indices are calm and near their highs, the European ones have already slipped under their 50-day averages, and Australia has broken its 200-day. None of them has yet fallen the way a real episode typically has by this point. Italy, the Nasdaq 100 and the euro area carry the most weekly risk, mostly because they are the most volatile.
