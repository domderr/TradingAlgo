October 2026 scenarios

October seasonality, stock correlation and the economic calendar put together to read the month ahead on the seven Mosaic markets. Work in progress, not a published model.

  • Market data to 25 September 2026
  • Index history from 1985 where available
  • Stocks last 10 years
  • Analysis run with AI, see below

In short

  • Long history is favourable, the last ten years are not. Over 19 to 41 Octobers (depending on the index) the month has been positive 62–71% of the time, with median returns between +1.9% and +3.3%. Over the last ten years the average runs from −0.6% to +0.9% and the share of positive Octobers falls as low as 40% (ASX 200). The long history also carries deep falls: −21.8% for the S&P 500 and −27.0% for the Nasdaq 100 in 1987, −14.7% for the Euro Stoxx 50 in 2008.
  • The day-of-month pattern is weak. The 12th trading day was positive in 8 of 10 years for the Nasdaq 100, the FTSE MIB and the ASX 200, but in only 3 of 9 for the DAX. Days 18–20 are negative most often in Australia (7 years of 10). With ten observations these are leads to check, not signals.
  • Stocks are moving little together. Average pairwise correlation over 30 sessions is between 0.06 (ASX 200) and 0.22 (FTSE MIB), below the five-year average everywhere. October is not a structurally different month for correlation: the gap to the rest of the year is at most 0.04.
  • The calendar is packed at both ends. First week: US jobs on 2 October. Last week: the Fed on the 28th, the ECB on the 29th, the Bank of Japan on 29–30 and US labour costs on the 30th. In between, US CPI on the 14th and the start of earnings season with JPMorgan on the 13th.
  • Three scenarios, with historical ranges. Down, middle and up, each tied to the average of the matching fifth of past Octobers. These are reference ranges, not forecasts: matching a scenario to today is a judgment, not a calculation.

What it says. For a given stock-level volatility, low correlation mechanically lowers index volatility, which fits the 21-day volatility being below its long-run average in all seven markets (scenario table). The data show no stable link with the direction of October: the sign is negative in 5 markets of 7 (down to −0.44 for the FTSE 100 and −0.40 for the FTSE MIB), meaning Octobers with more correlation tended to be worse Octobers, but with ten observations per market this is a hint, not a rule. Australia and the S&P 500 go the other way.

Scenarios for October on the seven markets

Three outcomes, each with the historical October return range of the index attached: down = average of the worst fifth of Octobers, middle = average of the middle 60%, up = average of the best fifth. The historical probability of each is therefore 20%, 60% and 20%. These are the only probabilities used.

Down

Rates and oil prevail.

  • The Fed hikes again on the 28th or signals more
  • US CPI on the 14th above expectations, strong jobs on 2 October
  • 10-year Treasury holding above 5.25%, oil rising again
  • Bank and tech earnings that do not absorb the cost of money

Middle

Markets looking for direction.

  • Mixed data, yields on a plateau near their highs
  • Fed and ECB as expected, no surprise on the path
  • Earnings good enough to keep indices in a narrow range
  • Low correlation: stock selection matters more than the market

Up

Relief on rates and oil.

  • Weak jobs on 2 October and softer CPI reduce hike bets
  • Hopes of Middle East progress push oil down
  • 10-year yields retrace
  • October closes in recovery, like the favourable half of history

On 18 September the European markets (FTSE 100 included) and the ASX 200 were already below their 50-day averages, while the S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100 were near their highs: a different starting point, not a forecast of which will hold up better.

How this analysis was done with AI

The analysis was carried out by Claude, an AI model from Anthropic (Sonnet 5), used inside Claude Code on the author's computer. The AI wrote and ran the scripts, searched the web and wrote the text. The questions, the direction and the decision to publish were the author's.

Step by step

  1. 1. Existing dataClaude looked in the TradingAlgo project for the seven markets and the daily prices of their stocks (a prices_daily.csv file per market). They started in January 2023: too short for seasonality.
  2. 2. Ten years of historyClaude reused the project's download script (yfinance, adjusted prices) with a separate output folder, without touching production data. A first attempt with a different call had failed entirely; it was fixed by using the original script and its HTTP session.
  3. 3. Seasonality and days of the monthMonthly and daily returns computed in Python on the stocks. For the long history the seven indices were also downloaded from 1985.
  4. 4. Pairwise correlationReuse of the "Stock correlation" note (30 sessions) and a new month-by-month computation on the stocks to compare October with the other months.
  5. 5. Calendar and contextWeb search for dates and news. US release dates were checked on the official BLS page; the others against several concordant search results.
  6. 6. Scenarios and textThe three-scenario structure, the ranges (historical fifths) and the commentary are a synthesis written by the AI from the numbers above.

What the code computed

Every seasonality, correlation, quintile, frequency and volatility figure on this page comes from Python scripts run on downloaded data, and the page was generated from those results, without retyping by hand. The same calculation can be repeated with the same inputs.

What is the AI's judgment

The definitions (trading days, equal-weight market, fifths as scenario ranges), the three scenario narratives and the way calendar events are tied to markets. No probability was assigned beyond the historical 20–60–20 frequency.

What was not verified, and known limits

  • Macro context from searches. Rates, the 5.23% yield and oil come from web-search summaries. One CNBC article could not be opened (error 403), so it was not read in full; these figures should be re-read at the source before use.
  • A conflict between sources. The date of US CPI was the 13th in one result and the 14th on the BLS page. The BLS date stands.
  • Survivorship bias. The stocks are today's members of each index taken back in time, and some tickers could not be downloaded. The index statistics do not have this problem, but their samples have different lengths.
  • Small samples. Ten Octobers for the stocks, 19 for the Euro Stoxx 50. A year like 2022 or 2018 moves the averages.
  • AI mistakes already found. In one daily chart the last point used too few years and made a line collapse artificially: it was spotted by looking at the result and corrected. Others may remain: the page was not reviewed by a second analyst.
  • Not advice. Research material, not a recommendation or a forecast.
